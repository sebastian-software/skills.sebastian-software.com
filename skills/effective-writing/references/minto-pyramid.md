# Minto Pyramid Analysis

Use the Minto Pyramid Principle to discover, test, or repair the logic of a
decision-oriented document before polishing its prose. Apply it when the user
asks for Minto, a pyramid, MECE, answer-first structure, a governing thought, or
a logic pressure test. It is especially useful for memos, recommendations,
proposals, reports, presentations, and explanatory arguments.

Do not force the method onto material whose effect depends on discovery,
chronology, scene, or ambiguity. Use the broader Structure and Story guidance
for personal essays, profiles, narrative reporting, and other pieces where a
claim-led hierarchy would erase the intended reading experience.

## Frame the reader's question

Name the reader, the decision or understanding they need, and the question the
document must answer. Use Situation, Complication, Question (SCQ) when it helps:

- **Situation:** State the stable context the reader can accept without debate.
- **Complication:** Name the change, tension, obstacle, or opportunity that makes
  the document necessary now.
- **Question:** State the question that the complication naturally raises for
  this reader.
- **Answer:** Put the document's governing point at the top of the pyramid.

Treat SCQ as a reasoning test, not a mandatory paragraph formula. Omit shared
context, reorder the introduction, or lead directly with the answer when the
reader already knows the situation and complication. Do not confuse top-down
logic with an absolute sentence-one rule: supply only the context needed for the
answer to make sense, then deliver it promptly.

## Build the pyramid

Build top-down when the likely answer is already clear. Build bottom-up when the
material is exploratory: list the meaningful points, group points that perform
the same logical job, state the implication of each group, and repeat until one
governing point emerges. Do not stop merely because the raw idea does not yet
compress into one sentence; use the grouping process to discover what the
writer can responsibly say.

### State the governing point

Write one specific answer to the reader's question. Make it a recommendation,
finding, conclusion, explanation, or instruction that the document can support.
Preserve qualifications that change its truth or scope. Do not require every
governing point to be provocative or universally certain.

Reject a top point that is only a topic label, an empty preview such as “There
are three reasons,” or a bundle of unrelated conclusions. If one sentence cannot
summarize the proposed branches without distortion, narrow the question or
split the document.

### Group supporting points

Make every parent point a real summary of the points immediately below it. At
each horizontal level:

1. Keep the children logically the same kind of idea.
2. Make them answer the same question about their parent.
3. Use one coherent relationship, inductive or deductive.
4. Put them in an order the reader can recognize.
5. Recurse until claims rest on adequate reasoning and evidence.

Use an **inductive group** for parallel reasons, findings, actions, effects, or
parts. Name the plural noun that describes every child, then order the group by
time or cause, structural order, or rank. If no single plural noun fits, the
group probably mixes logical kinds.

Use a **deductive group** for a linked argument whose points depend on one
another, such as rule, case, implication. Keep the chain short and preserve the
dependency; do not present dependent steps as parallel reasons.

Prefer a small, readable group, but do not impose exactly three branches, a
fixed depth, or one evidence item per branch. Let the question and material
determine the useful number of points. Regroup a crowded level instead of
flattening the whole document.

### Apply MECE precisely

Test sibling points as mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive relative
to their parent question and the declared scope:

- **Mutually exclusive:** Each sibling performs distinct logical work. Remove
  double-counting, disguised restatement, and categories that overlap on the
  dimension being used.
- **Collectively exhaustive:** Together the siblings are sufficient to support
  the parent point for this assignment. Address a missing necessary reason,
  step, part, or material counterargument.

Do not interpret “collectively exhaustive” as covering everything imaginable
about the topic. State scope boundaries openly. When the material does not form
a strict partition, prefer an honest set of distinct, sufficient reasons over a
false claim of mathematical exhaustiveness.

### Place evidence under claims

Place supplied or verified facts, data, examples, mechanisms, and relevant
counterevidence under the claim they support. Keep reasoning visible between
evidence and conclusion; a vivid example does not prove prevalence, and a
statistic does not explain its own implication.

Use as much evidence as the claim needs. Label missing proof and uncertainty
instead of fabricating a statistic, quotation, case, source, or experience.
Preserve conditions and limitations near the branch they qualify.

## Pressure-test the logic

Run these tests at every level that matters:

- **Reader-question test:** Does the top point answer the question this reader
  actually has?
- **Summary test:** Is each parent derived from and no broader than its children?
- **Question-and-answer test:** Does each child answer a natural “why,” “how,” or
  “what” question raised by its parent?
- **Kind test:** Are siblings comparable ideas rather than a mixture of reasons,
  examples, steps, and conclusions?
- **Order test:** Can the reader infer the ordering principle without guessing?
- **MECE test:** Do siblings avoid duplicate work and cover what the parent
  requires within scope?
- **Support test:** Does each consequential claim have adequate evidence and
  reasoning, with uncertainty carried upward?
- **Necessity test:** Would removing this node weaken the answer? If not, cut it,
  move it to context, or keep it outside the document.

For an existing draft, create a reverse outline first. Map each paragraph or
section to a pyramid node, identify the closest existing governing point, and
mark material that supports no node or tries to serve multiple siblings. Use
that map to distinguish missing logic from weak prose.

## Shape the introduction and document

Use the pyramid to determine hierarchy, not to make every paragraph sound like a
consulting slide. Introduce only enough situation and complication to activate
the reader's question. Then state the answer and unfold its support in the order
the pyramid established.

For a revision, specify which sections to move, merge, split, qualify, support,
or cut. Rewrite the full piece only when the user asks. Keep voice and
sentence-level editing downstream, but treat evidence, scope, and necessary
qualification as structural concerns rather than optional polish.

## Return a usable analysis

Match the requested artifact. Unless the user asks for a finished rewrite or a
specific visual format, return:

1. **Reader question:** the question the document must answer
2. **Governing point:** the answer at the top
3. **Pyramid:** an indented tree of supporting groups and evidence
4. **Logic audit:** the highest-impact summary, kind, order, MECE, or support
   failures
5. **Restructuring plan:** concrete moves in execution order
6. **Evidence gaps:** proof to verify or obtain, without invented substitutes

Add an exact opener when it materially helps. Generate HTML, Mermaid, or another
visual artifact only when the user requests it or the hierarchy is too complex
to judge in plain Markdown. Keep the analysis inspectable and editable; the
method should clarify the thinking rather than hide it inside presentation.

## Method basis

- [Barbara Minto's official overview](https://barbaraminto.com/) defines the
  pyramid as ideas organized under a single point and describes SCQ as a way to
  identify the question in the reader's mind.
- [McKinsey's interview with Barbara Minto](https://www.mckinsey.com/alumni/news-and-events/global-news/alumni-news/barbara-minto-mece-i-invented-it-so-i-get-to-say-how-to-pronounce-it)
  records the three logical rules: summarize upward, group like ideas, and order
  them logically, together with the MECE test.
- [Pearson's overview of the third edition](https://www.pearson.com/en-gb/subject-catalog/p/the-pyramid-principle/P200000015259/9781292763255)
  confirms the method's focus on organizing thinking before writing and its
  distinction between induction and deduction.
