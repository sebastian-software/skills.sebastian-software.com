# Positioning and Segmentation

Use this route to decide which market situation to serve, what alternative the
product must replace, which category context helps people understand it, and
which differentiated value the available evidence supports.

## Start with the decision

State:

- the product and maturity
- the market, geography, language, and time horizon
- the positioning artifact or decision required
- the accountable owner and decision date
- the product and commercial constraints that cannot be changed here
- the evidence cutoff and important missing inputs

Do not write the final position before clarifying whether the task is choosing
a segment, choosing a category context, reviewing current positioning, or
translating an already approved product direction.

## Build the evidence register

Separate:

- observed customer situations, triggers, alternatives, and decisions
- product behavior and constraints verified in the supported experience
- measured customer outcomes and adoption patterns
- sales, support, interview, and win/loss accounts with source and context
- current competitor or market facts with date and provenance
- stakeholder interpretations and strategic commitments
- assumptions and unknowns

Do not infer a shared need from a company label, job title, demographic trait,
or market-size estimate alone. Do not turn internal product language into a
customer quotation.

## Find decision-relevant segments

A useful segment groups customers whose situation leads to a meaningfully
similar product and go-to-market decision. Compare candidate groups by:

- triggering situation and urgency
- desired progress and consequence of doing nothing
- current alternative and switching burden
- buyer, user, approver, and blocker roles
- evaluation and purchase conditions
- product requirements and time to value
- proof and trust requirements
- delivery, support, risk, and economic fit
- acquisition access and expected adoption path

Prefer the narrowest segment that is specific enough to change the product-
marketing decision and large enough to learn from. A segment is not useful when
its members require contradictory promises, proof, purchase motions, or product
behavior.

Distinguish:

- **target segment:** the group the current decision prioritizes
- **adjacent segment:** plausible later scope requiring separate evidence
- **excluded segment:** deliberately not served by this position
- **unknown segment:** potentially relevant but not sufficiently understood

Do not use an arbitrary score or universal threshold to make the choice look
objective. Compare evidence, strategic fit, economics, access, risk, and
opportunity cost transparently.

## Identify the real alternative

Position against what the target customer would otherwise do:

- continue the current workflow
- combine manual work and general tools
- buy or build another product
- delegate the problem to a service provider
- accept the consequence and do nothing

Ask what causes reconsideration of the current alternative and what makes
switching costly or risky. A named competitor is relevant only when customers
actually evaluate it in the selected situation.

Record each alternative's strengths as well as weaknesses. Do not create a
straw opponent to make the product appear differentiated.

## Choose the category context

Category language helps the audience predict what the product is, when it is
relevant, and what alternatives to compare. Consider:

- an established category the audience already understands
- a narrower use-case or workflow description
- a combination of familiar categories
- a new category only when existing language creates a material
  misunderstanding that the team can afford to correct

Compare each option by:

- speed and accuracy of comprehension
- expectations it creates about features, price, proof, and buying process
- relevant alternatives it brings into view
- constraints or misconceptions it imports
- education and distribution cost
- truthfulness across the supported product

Do not invent a category merely to sound unique. Novel terminology creates work;
it is not customer value or evidence of differentiation.

## Establish differentiated value

For each candidate difference, trace:

```text
product mechanism -> customer consequence -> evidence -> constraint
```

A useful differentiator is:

- relevant to the selected customer's decision
- specific enough to compare
- supported by product behavior and appropriate customer evidence
- durable enough for the decision horizon
- difficult for the real alternative to neutralize without a tradeoff

Distinguish a product capability from the customer consequence it may enable.
Do not claim an outcome when only the mechanism has been verified. Do not claim
uniqueness without current comparative evidence.

## Develop positioning options

Create two to four materially different options when the choice is open. Vary a
decision that matters, such as:

- target situation or segment
- alternative displaced
- category context
- primary value and product mechanism
- proof burden or commercial motion

For each option state:

1. target customer and triggering situation
2. current alternative and reason to change
3. category context
4. differentiated value and product mechanism
5. evidence, contradictions, and unknowns
6. product, commercial, delivery, and trust constraints
7. audience that becomes a non-goal
8. smallest test that could disqualify the option

Do not produce cosmetic rewrites of the same position and present them as
strategic alternatives.

## Write the positioning brief

Return:

- decision scope and evidence cutoff
- target segment, situation, and buying context
- customer progress and current alternatives
- chosen category context and comprehension tradeoff
- differentiated value, mechanism, proof, and constraints
- rejected options and explicit non-goals
- known objections and evidence gaps
- downstream message and channel implications
- owner, decision window, and reopening conditions

Keep this brief as an internal decision artifact. It supplies messaging but is
not itself homepage copy, a slogan, a campaign, or proof.

## Quality Check

- Does the segment change a meaningful product-marketing decision?
- Is the position tied to a situation and alternative rather than a broad label?
- Does the category improve truthful comprehension?
- Can each difference be traced to mechanism, evidence, and constraints?
- Are excluded audiences and rejected options explicit?
- Would contradictory evidence change the recommendation?
