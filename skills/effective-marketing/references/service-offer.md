# Build a Service Offer

## Choose the customer and problem

Start with observed customer situations. What frustrates the buyer, which
symptoms can they recognize, what have they already tried, and what does the
unresolved problem cost them? Separate customer statements from your
assumptions. Define the segment so that a researcher could find prospects and a
writer could address them specifically. Industry alone is seldom enough; include
company size or life stage, buyer role, trigger event, location when relevant,
and exclusion criteria.

Evaluate each customer and problem pair:

1. Is the problem urgent and recognizable to the buyer?
2. Can this buyer authorize and implement a solution?
3. Can the provider deliver the proposed outcome with available capabilities?
4. Is there a realistic path to initial conversations?

## Seven offer dimensions

| Dimension | Decision to make |
| --- | --- |
| Customer | Who is this for, and who should be excluded? |
| Transformation | What observable change should occur? |
| Price | What is included, what is charged, and when is payment due? |
| Mechanism | Which steps plausibly create the outcome? |
| Time | When does work begin, and when can progress be checked? |
| Quality | How will good delivery be assessed? |
| Customer effort | What data, decisions, and participation are required? |

Draft an internal offer statement: “For [specific customer] facing [recognized
problem], we aim to achieve [verifiable outcome] through [mechanism] within
[realistic scope or period], provided [customer contribution].” This is a
working definition, not a public claim or guarantee.

## Five decisions in sequence

1. **Whom to serve:** List up to three reachable segments where the team has
   knowledge, access, or results. Record role, size, trigger, buying ability,
   and proof for each.
2. **Which problem:** Gather concrete cases from conversations and projects.
   Distinguish symptom, suspected cause, and desired result. A problem belongs
   at the center of an offer only when customers will spend time or money to
   address it.
3. **How to deliver:** Break work into assessment, setup, implementation,
   feedback, and stabilization. Identify dependencies and customer
   participation.
4. **Which format:** Compare advisory work, collaborative implementation, fully
   managed service, bounded project, and ongoing support. The work required
   determines the format.
5. **Which price hypothesis:** Estimate delivery cost and capacity, assess value
   to this customer, then test a defensible price rather than borrowing a fixed
   number from another market.

Describe the entry point, diagnosis, implementation, feedback, and completion.
Standardize repeatable parts while leaving room for genuine customer needs.
Document delivery effort and the maximum number of concurrent clients.

## Compare offer ideas

Score each criterion from 1 to 5, attaching evidence or labeling an open
assumption. A sum supports judgment; it never offsets a fatal delivery or
customer-fit problem.

| Criterion | Strong signal |
| --- | --- |
| Problem strength | Repeated pain, observable cost, timely reason to act |
| Reachability | Known communities, search terms, directories, or existing relationships |
| Buying ability | Plausible budget or personally meaningful value |
| Deliverability | Skills, resources, access, and customer cooperation are available |
| Proof | Result can be shown with data, a sample, or a case |
| Repeatability | Similar cases can follow a shared core process |

```text
Available proof / untested assumptions:

First market-validation action:
```

For B2B, examine the cost of the problem, available resources, and the buying
process. For consumer services, personal value, affordability, and customer time
may matter more than an asserted return on investment. For design, IT, and other
creative work, distinguish one-time production from maintenance. A retainer
needs defined recurring work and a clear reason to continue.

## Diagnose weak demand

Wrong contacts suggest a segment or channel problem. Many conversations with
little relevance suggest a problem-definition or message problem. Interest
without purchase calls for examining qualification, value, price, and the
decision process. Sales without good outcomes call for repairing delivery and
customer fit before increasing demand.
