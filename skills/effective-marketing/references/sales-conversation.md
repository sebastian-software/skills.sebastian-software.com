# Consultative Sales Conversation

## Prepare

Read the qualification notes, list open decision points, and consider only
solutions the team can actually deliver. Agree on purpose and duration. A guide
keeps the discussion complete; it does not replace listening.

## Conversation arc

1. **Frame:** Agree on purpose, time, and what decision can be reached.
2. **Current state:** Understand the process, resources, and previous attempts.
3. **Problem:** Explore impact, urgency, and obstacles.
4. **Goal:** Define what a good change would look like and how it could be
   observed.
5. **Fit:** Recheck prerequisites, decision process, budget, and participation.
6. **Mechanism:** Explain the parts of the service that address this customer's
   problem, with supportable examples.
7. **Offer:** State outcome, scope, boundaries, timing, responsibilities, price,
   and terms.
8. **Decision:** Resolve questions and agree on an honest next step.

Link every part of the method to a need the customer actually named. A case
explains its starting point, work, and outcome; it does not guarantee the same
result here.

## Prepare the price conversation

1. Ask the buyer to describe the desired outcome and the cost of leaving the
   problem unresolved.
2. Restate the proposed scope, timeline, and required participation.
3. State the price, payment schedule, and included work plainly.
4. Allow questions. If the buyer says it is too expensive, determine whether the
   issue is cash flow, unclear value, trust, urgency, authority, or a competing
   option.
5. Change price only with a reasoned change in scope, schedule, payment terms,
   or risk allocation.

Avoid inventing a return-on-investment calculation. If a financial comparison is
useful, show the inputs, uncertainty, and who supplied them.

## Seven conditions for a sound decision

The customer recognizes a meaningful problem, believes improvement is possible,
sees a reason to act, considers outside help useful, has necessary resources,
trusts the provider, and understands why this particular offer fits. If one
condition is missing, more slides rarely solve it. Ask about the gap, provide
relevant evidence, or end the sales path when the fit is genuinely absent.

Trust has several layers: the person conducting the conversation, the company,
the service, and the customer's own ability to participate. Past disappointments
and implementation barriers may matter as much as credentials.

## Diagnose objections

1. Restate the concern neutrally and ask what it means in this case.
2. Determine whether it is the only open issue.
3. Locate the cause: missing information, resources, priority, risk, trust, or
   poor fit.
4. Address that cause with explanation, evidence, or a sensible scope change.
5. Check whether the concern is resolved and accept a no.

“I need to think” may mean internal approval, low trust, or low priority; an
automatic discount would be arbitrary. If the buyer has an existing provider,
ask whether the goal is being met and what a switch would require. Satisfaction
with the current solution is a legitimate outcome.

## Review and practice

For role-play, choose one customer situation, objection type, and observation
goal. Ask afterward whether the problem was understood, prerequisites were
checked, the offer was specific, and the decision was free and clear. Adjust the
weakest part of the guide first.

Track qualified meetings, attendance, proposals, wins, and recurring objections
by offer and segment. A low close rate may originate upstream: poor-fit leads,
shallow diagnosis, unclear scope, or expectations set by marketing. Change one
likely bottleneck and practice it deliberately.
