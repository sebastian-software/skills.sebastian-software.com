# Video and Audio Content

Use this for long video, podcasts, audio versions, and short video clips,
including their briefs, production, and reuse. Text posts and platform treatment
stay with the social and LinkedIn references.

## Long video and audio

Choose a title based on a real question or search intent. The opening should
name that question and the benefit of staying, without a long preamble. A
reliable outline is problem, common mistake, diagnosis, example, approach, and
next step. Put a working contact path in the description or profile. An audio
version of a video works only when visual examples are explained verbally.

Evaluate a video title with four signals:

1. Who is it for: role, situation, or business stage?
2. What requirement screens out unsuitable viewers?
3. Is the subject relevant to a buyer's decision?
4. Which decision is the viewer trying to make?

Possible structures include “Before [role] chooses [tool or service]: [decisive
question]” and “Why [symptom] persists despite [previous attempt].” The content
must fulfill the title's promise. Click-through and retention can diagnose the
presentation; qualified inquiries determine business usefulness.

## Short video

Make one point. Start with a recognizable observation or tension in the first
seconds, then explain the cause or one practical step. Use plain language, real
scenes, readable captions, and clear audio. A clip from a longer recording needs
its own beginning and ending. The profile and pinned material should make the
next step easy.

```text
Opening seconds: specific observation or tension
Context: who experiences this and why?
Core: one insight or practical step
Example: brief scene, verified number, or real case
Close: implication and, when appropriate, one action
```

Check whether the opening works without sound, captions fit a phone screen, and
music does not obscure speech. Make specialist material accessible without
stripping out the detail that makes it useful to the buyer.

## Brief before production

Record the intended customer, offer connection, one recognizable symptom, main
claim, supporting example, channel, and desired action. Draft the title or hook
and an outline. Use bullet prompts for natural speech; use exact wording where
precision, claims, or compliance require it.

## Production sequence

1. Check the topic and title against the intended customer and actual offer.
2. Divide the argument into a few sections.
3. Write the opening and ending separately.
4. Record and review speech, image, and comprehensibility.
5. Remove slow preambles and repetition.
6. Check captions, links, mobile readability, and export.

For a long video, settle a title hypothesis and target viewer, collect three to
five main points, record, remove irrelevant sections, and verify description and
call to action. For an audio version, explain everything that the video shows
visually. Identify independent claims and examples for short clips; give each a
fresh opening and close.

## Reuse without dumping the same file everywhere

Extract self-contained arguments, examples, and objections from long recordings.
Adapt an argument into a text post, short video, or part of a longer explanation
as appropriate. Rewrite the opening, language, and next action for the
destination channel. A clip must make sense without the original introduction. A
transcript can support drafting but should be edited for clarity rather than
published raw.

## Weekly operating rhythm

Reserve separate blocks for topic selection, briefing, recording or drafting,
editing, and publishing. Choose a frequency the team can maintain. Consistent
useful output is better than an ambitious schedule abandoned after two weeks.

```text
Working title and intended viewer:
Question answered:
One-sentence main claim:
Claims that must not be made:
Example, data, and usage permissions:
Outline or script:
Visual and audio requirements:
Edit points and possible short clips:
Caption and aspect-ratio requirements:
Call to action and destination:
Reviewer and acceptance criteria:
```

## Format checks

- **Text:** Does the first visible line say something meaningful? Does each
  paragraph develop one idea? Is the action unambiguous?
- **Long video:** Does the answer begin early? Do title, thumbnail, and opening
  create the same expectation? Is evidence understandable?
- **Short video:** Does the first moment work muted? Are captions readable on a
  phone? Is there only one main claim?
- **Podcast:** Are gestures and screen views described? Is audio level
  consistent?

Before publication, confirm that the title is fulfilled, cases and statements
are true and authorized, the right audience is addressed, and the next step
works. Afterward, separate production failures from topic failures: poor audio
can sink a strong idea; broad attention from unsuitable viewers points to the
title or hook; suitable viewers who never inquire may indicate an offer,
profile, or call-to-action issue. Log one concrete change for the next piece.
