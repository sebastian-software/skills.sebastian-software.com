# Service Growth Operations

## Build in five phases

1. **Assess the starting point:** Available time, capabilities, target
   customers, financial runway, existing relationships, and commitments.
   Separate facts from assumptions.
2. **Define problem and offer:** Identify a reachable customer, meaningful
   problem, deliverable result, service method, and price logic. Make the next
   step clear enough for a prospect to respond.
3. **Test real demand:** Speak with suitable people, record recurring problems,
   and test a bounded offer. Count substantive conversations and commitments,
   not polite approval of an idea.
4. **Deliver reliably:** Define start, customer participation, milestones,
   quality, and completion. Gather publishable evidence only with permission.
   Fix delivery failures before increasing lead volume.
5. **Make it repeatable:** Document outreach, qualification, sales, and
   delivery. Add tools and roles after the process and bottleneck are
   understood.

Each phase should end with a documented state, decision, and next action.
Branding can strengthen an offer that works; it cannot establish demand by
itself.

## Set up the business responsibly

Create an owner-and-date checklist for registration or entity setup, invoicing,
banking, accounting, contracts, tax, insurance, privacy, and digital contact
paths. Requirements depend on country, region, entity, and service. Use current
official guidance and qualified local advice for actual legal or tax decisions.
Do not treat a checklist from another country as a complete setup plan.

## Find the bottleneck

Map suitable people reached → conversations → qualified opportunities → sales →
good delivery → referrals or repeat work. Mark the weakest transition or
greatest uncertainty. Check data quality first, then process and message.

| Symptom | First area to inspect |
| --- | --- |
| Few suitable conversations | Segment, offer, visibility, outreach |
| Conversations without purchases | Qualification, value, trust, price |
| Sales without good outcomes | Capacity, method, customer participation, quality |
| Overload despite revenue | Standardization, priorities, delegation |

Form a testable hypothesis: “If we make [change] for [segment], [observable
signal] should improve within [period].” Choose the next task by expected
impact, effort, dependencies, and risk. Targeted customer conversations may
precede a major website project when the offer is still poorly understood. If
sales are already exceeding delivery capacity, repair delivery before increasing
ads.

## One-week decision sheet

```text
Current bottleneck:
Counts or observations supporting it:
One hypothesis:
Up to three actions:
Owner and completion date for each:
Signal and date for review:
Legal or tax questions requiring local review:
```

Change one major assumption at a time. A week of activity is useful only if it
produces an outcome or learning that changes the next decision.

## Plan the week in six steps

1. Collect commitments in one list and define an outcome for each.
2. Block fixed client meetings and delivery deadlines first.
3. Select a few tasks that directly address the current business bottleneck.
4. Batch similar work: research, conversations, creation, follow-up, and
   administration.
5. Leave capacity for client work, new inquiries, and disruptions.
6. Review delivered work, business effect, and deferrals at week's end.

Reserve “urgent” for genuinely time-sensitive consequences. Urgency alone does
not prove business impact. Divide large tasks into observable intermediate
results that fit a calendar block. If a task cannot begin, name the missing
input or decision.

## Run a simple board

Use inbox, planned, in progress, waiting, and done. Each card needs an outcome,
owner, due date, next action, and relevant dependency. Limit active work. Mark
blocked tasks with reason and review date. “Done” means the output was accepted
or handed off; sending something may be only an intermediate step. Keep context
and decisions in notes, but avoid a second uncontrolled task list.

For repeated work, provide a short checklist. Example inquiry handling: inspect
the incoming request, read its source and promise, contact the person, record
the outcome, and set the next task. Measure actual duration so future scheduling
improves.

## Delegable procedure

```text
Purpose and desired outcome:
Trigger:
Required inputs and access:
Steps and decision points:
Quality standard and acceptance check:
Scope of independent authority:
Deadline and handoff location:
Escalation condition and contact:
Good example and common failure:
```

Start with repeatable work that has a clear quality measure. Run one example
together, then a guided attempt, then independent execution. Measure quality and
rework as well as time saved. Delegate decisions only where the person has the
context, access, and authority. Repeated questions point to missing inputs or
instructions. Variable quality calls for finding the failing step, not
withdrawing the entire responsibility.

Automate only when the normal rule and exceptions are understood. A workflow
that cannot be explained or checked manually is unlikely to become reliable
merely by adding software.

## Weekly review

Ask: What was delivered? Which work visibly improved demand, sales, or customer
outcomes? Where did waiting or rework occur? Which single rule should change
next week? Record the decision and owner. If a calendar block repeatedly fails,
revisit priority, duration, prerequisites, or interruptions rather than asking
only for more discipline.
