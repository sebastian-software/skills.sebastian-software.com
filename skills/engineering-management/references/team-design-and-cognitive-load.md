# Team Design and Cognitive Load

Use this reference when team boundaries, specialist dependencies, staffing
constraints, or a reorganization are under consideration.

## Establish the Driver

Team design should respond to durable work interactions. Collect:

- customer or operational flows the group must own;
- dependencies and their frequency, latency, and coordination cost;
- knowledge concentration and interruption demand;
- change coupling across systems and disciplines;
- cognitive load from domain complexity, tools, operations, and process;
- capacity, priority, and skill constraints;
- risks and obligations that require separation or oversight.

Do not infer a structural problem from missed dates alone. Overcommitment,
unclear priorities, weak product evidence, unstable architecture, or one
temporary project can produce similar symptoms.

## Distinguish Cognitive Load

Consider three forms without pretending they are precisely measurable:

- **intrinsic:** complexity inherent in the domain or problem;
- **supporting:** learning and reasoning that builds useful capability;
- **avoidable:** fragmented tools, unnecessary handoffs, unstable interfaces,
  ambiguous ownership, or process overhead.

Ask affected people where context is lost, which responsibilities cannot be
held together reliably, and which interruptions prevent sustained work.

## Prefer the Smallest Change

Before redrawing teams, test:

- a clearer service or decision boundary;
- an explicit collaboration agreement;
- a time-boxed embedded specialist;
- protected support rotations;
- documentation and pairing to reduce single-person dependency;
- narrowed scope or sequenced priorities;
- a platform or enabling investment justified by repeated demand.

Use a reorganization only when evidence suggests a durable boundary mismatch
and smaller interventions cannot reasonably address it.

## Evaluate a Proposed Structure

For each option describe:

- work and outcomes each team can own end to end;
- interactions that become easier and harder;
- new dependencies, queues, and leadership load;
- knowledge transfer and operational continuity;
- effects on career paths, inclusion, location, and psychological safety;
- migration cost and reversible checkpoints.

Do not prescribe a universal team size, ratio, topology, or organizational
pattern. Names and diagrams are hypotheses about interaction, not proof.

## Manage the Transition

A responsible transition needs:

1. a stated driver and success signals;
2. consultation with affected people before irreversible commitments;
3. explicit interim ownership for customers, systems, incidents, and decisions;
4. capacity for knowledge transfer;
5. risks, safeguards, and escalation owner;
6. a review date and willingness to adjust.

Do not hide cost through forced overtime or assume people are interchangeable.
Stop when employment terms, compensation, location obligations, or legal duties
determine the change.

## Deliverable

Return evidence for the driver, current interaction map, options and tradeoffs,
the smallest recommended change, human and operational safeguards, transition
ownership, and review conditions.
