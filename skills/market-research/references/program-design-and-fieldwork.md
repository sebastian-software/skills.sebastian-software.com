# Program Design and Fieldwork

Use primary research when the decision depends on behavior, context, language,
motivation, sequence, or decision dynamics that existing records cannot answer.

## Build the research brief

Record:

- the decision and accountable owner
- the people, situation, market, time horizon, and exclusions
- current evidence, assumptions, contradictions, and missing perspectives
- learning questions and what each answer could change
- method, participant or unit, sample logic, source, and analysis plan
- consent, recording, incentive, accessibility, access, retention, and deletion
- timing, owner, dependencies, risks, and stop conditions
- the required evidence register and downstream handoff

Write questions that can produce discriminating evidence. Replace “Do users
like the idea?” with questions about the last relevant event, current workflow,
alternatives, consequences, and actual commitments. Include at least one
question or case that could weaken the preferred explanation.

## Choose a method for the uncertainty

| Uncertainty | Useful methods | Important limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Situation, trigger, job, language, or decision sequence | Recent-event interview, artifact review | Accounts are retrospective |
| Tacit workflow, interruption, coordination, or environment | Contextual inquiry, observation, task reconstruction | Presence and setup may change behavior |
| Change across time | Diary study, longitudinal interview, behavior log | Participation and attrition shape the record |
| Comprehension or interaction | Realistic task observation, prototype or usability study | Prototype response is not market demand |
| Distribution in a defined population | Survey with defensible sampling and measures | Convenience responses do not establish prevalence |
| Purchase, switching, or commitment | Buying-process interview plus observed commercial behavior | Stated willingness is not payment |
| Market structure or alternatives | Secondary research plus buyer and seller inquiry | Published categories may not match customer choice |

Combine methods only when each resolves a different uncertainty. More methods
do not compensate for a vague decision.

## Sample for relevance and variation

Define inclusion and exclusion criteria from the decision. Recruit people with
recent relevant experience rather than people who can merely imagine it.
Capture meaningful variations such as:

- buyer, user, payer, approver, administrator, and blocker roles
- current alternative and degree of switching
- successful, failed, abandoned, retained, and churned outcomes
- first-time and experienced behavior
- product, plan, channel, geography, language, accessibility, or risk context

Do not claim statistical representativeness from a small qualitative sample.
Do not stop at a universal interview count. Continue, narrow, or stop according
to decision risk, participant variation, contradictory evidence, information
gain, access, and the cost of another session. Record who is missing and why.

## Recruit and screen without leading

Use a short screener that:

1. establishes recent relevant behavior or decision participation;
2. verifies role, context, and the variation needed in the sample;
3. excludes conflicts or conditions that would make participation unsafe;
4. avoids revealing the preferred answer or product promise;
5. records recruitment channel, incentive, selection, and rejection reason.

Separate research recruitment from sales qualification. Do not disguise a pitch
as an interview or make product access conditional on praise.

## Protect participant control

Explain purpose, format, duration, incentive, recording, note taking, data use,
access, retention, deletion, and contact details in plain language. Obtain the
required consent before recording. Make it possible to skip questions, pause,
withdraw, or request deletion where applicable.

Collect only what the decision needs. Separate identifiers from working
observations, restrict access, and avoid durable artifacts that expose
unnecessary personal or sensitive detail. Test recording, permissions,
accessibility, backup notes, and facilitator responsibilities before the first
session.

## Conduct behavioral interviews

Organize a guide around learning areas, not a script that must be read verbatim:

1. ordinary context and the participant's role
2. the most recent relevant event
3. trigger and prior state
4. sequence, tools, artifacts, people, interruptions, and workarounds
5. alternatives considered or used
6. consequences, tradeoffs, anxieties, and desired progress
7. commitment, adoption, repeated value, or abandonment
8. what the interview missed

Use neutral prompts such as “What happened next?”, “Show me how that worked,”
and “What did that mean in practice?” Avoid asking participants to validate the
team's diagnosis, predict use of a hypothetical feature, rank abstractions
without context, or solve the product problem for the team.

For win/loss work, reconstruct the complete decision set, participants,
evidence, changes of direction, non-decision, and outcome after the choice.
Choose an interviewer and setting that reduce pressure to protect the
relationship or justify the sale.

## Observe work and experience

When action matters, record discrete evidence:

- action and sequence
- artifact, object, tool, state, or handoff
- environmental, social, accessibility, and time condition
- interruption, error, workaround, adaptation, or recovery
- quote tied to the moment
- visible consequence or result

Keep observation and interpretation separate. Use realistic content, tasks,
permissions, states, and interruptions. A usability study supports an
experience decision; it does not by itself establish market demand or
willingness to adopt.

## Design surveys proportionately

Use a survey when a defined population and measured construct justify it, not
because an interview is harder to schedule.

- Define the construct and how each item measures it.
- Use response options that are mutually intelligible and include legitimate
  non-applicable or unknown states.
- Avoid double-barreled, leading, loaded, and hypothetical questions.
- Pilot comprehension and completion before launch.
- Record invitation channel, denominator, response and completion patterns,
  missing data, exclusions, weighting, and analysis choices.
- Report counts and denominators with uncertainty appropriate to the sample.

Open-text responses can reveal language and explanations. They do not repair a
biased sampling frame or turn a convenience survey into population evidence.

## Fieldwork handoff

Return:

1. research brief and versioned instruments
2. sample and recruitment register
3. consent and data-handling record
4. session or response log with exclusions and deviations
5. raw-material locations and access limits
6. observation register ready for synthesis
7. participant coverage gaps and fieldwork incidents
