# Preparation and Options

Load this reference before a consequential negotiation, especially when the
parties' interests, alternatives, authority, or exchangeable terms are unclear.

## Define the Decision System

Record:

- parties, representatives, affected stakeholders, and relationship horizon
- who uses, pays, approves, signs, implements, supports, or can veto
- the decision process, expected stages, dates, and required reviews
- what may be discussed, proposed, recommended, approved, or signed by each
  participant
- confidential information and what may be shared
- the source and verification date for consequential facts

Do not infer authority from seniority, attendance, confidence, or a job title.
Ask directly when the mandate is unclear.

## Separate Positions From Interests

For each party, distinguish:

| Layer | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Position | What term or action are they currently requesting? |
| Interest | What outcome, risk, pressure, or opportunity sits beneath it? |
| Constraint | What budget, policy, timing, capability, or approval limits them? |
| Preference | What matters but could be exchanged? |
| Unknown | What must be learned rather than assumed? |

Use current statements and observable facts. Treat any account of the other
party's motives as a hypothesis until they confirm or correct it.

## Build Real Alternatives

An alternative is an action available if no agreement is reached. List:

- concrete action and owner
- earliest usable date
- cash, time, switching, operational, and relationship cost
- dependencies and approvals
- probability of success
- value and risk compared with the proposed agreement

Strengthen alternatives through legitimate preparation: obtain a real quote,
reduce dependency, phase scope, build a fallback, secure internal approval, or
change timing. Never manufacture an offer, buyer, deadline, policy, technical
constraint, or ability to walk away.

Do not confuse an aspiration with an alternative. “Find another supplier” is
not usable until candidates, time, costs, and transition risks are credible.

## Set Targets, Limits, and Authority

Write four separate boundaries:

1. **Target:** an ambitious, explainable outcome.
2. **Acceptable range:** outcomes worth considering against real alternatives.
3. **Stop or escalation boundary:** a term that requires pause, rejection, or
   additional review.
4. **Mandate:** what the participant may explore, propose, recommend, approve,
   sign, or communicate.

Support ranges with customer, financial, product, technical, or operational
evidence. Do not use an arbitrary score or universal percentage.

Define what happens at the boundary: ask for time, introduce the decision
owner, narrow scope, change another term, or end the conversation respectfully.

## Create Exchangeable Dimensions

Move beyond a single issue. Relevant dimensions may include:

- price, billing unit, package, volume, payment timing, term, and renewal
- scope, sequence, quality bar, acceptance criteria, and change control
- delivery timing, dependencies, staffing assumptions, and implementation help
- service levels, support hours, escalation, reporting, and remedies
- risk allocation, caps, warranties, security review, and reversibility
- exclusivity, territory, data rights, publicity, case-study, or reference use
- pilot size, success criteria, decision date, expansion, and exit

Only include a dimension the parties can legitimately discuss and an authorized
owner can approve. Route price and package design through
`pricing-and-packaging`, product commitments through `product-management`, and
technical feasibility through `software-architecture`.

## Design Coherent Packages

Prepare two or three packages with materially different tradeoffs. For each:

- explain the customer and business outcome
- state every conditional exchange
- estimate cost, value, risk, and operational load
- identify evidence and assumptions
- mark terms outside the current mandate
- define the approval and execution path

Avoid a decoy package that no reasonable party should choose. Options should
reveal priorities and create legitimate choice, not manipulate consent.

## Prepare an Information Plan

List questions that could change the proposal:

- Which underlying outcome is most important, and why now?
- What would make an agreement unusable?
- Who else must approve or implement it?
- Which timing, budget, scope, risk, or policy constraints are fixed?
- What alternatives are being considered, if the party is willing to share?
- Which terms are easy for them to change and costly for us, or vice versa?
- What evidence would resolve the disputed fact or technical assumption?

Decide what information can be shared, what needs permission, and what must
remain confidential. Giving a truthful reason does not require disclosing every
internal detail.

## Preparation Deliverable

Return:

1. negotiation statement and decision process
2. facts, assumptions, unknowns, and verification needs
3. interests, constraints, and hypotheses by party
4. real alternatives and their comparative value
5. target, acceptable range, escalation boundary, and mandate
6. exchangeable dimensions and coherent packages
7. questions, disclosure limits, and approval checkpoints
