# Service Pilots and Proof

Use a pilot to reduce one named uncertainty about a service offer: customer
problem, segment, willingness to buy, delivery ability, or outcome. Select
customers who could later buy the regular offer.

## State the uncertainty

Write: “For [customer type], [service] addresses [problem] and should produce
[observable outcome] when [conditions] hold.” Decide in advance which
observation would strengthen the claim and which would weaken it. A useful pilot
studies a real customer problem under conditions resembling future paid work.

Test four uncertainties separately: **problem** (do suitable customers describe
it as important?), **offer** (do they understand the result, process, and
contribution?), **delivery** (can the team produce a meaningful result?), and
**purchase** (would they pay for the next stage, and for what?). If the
audience, service, and message all change at once, the outcome is hard to
interpret. Version each one.

## Select suitable pilot customers

- They experience the relevant problem now.
- Buying authority, budget, data access, and willingness to participate are at
  least plausible.
- They resemble a segment that can later be reached commercially.
- The work fits the available time and skills.

A poor-fit pilot can fail despite a sound method. Testing only with
exceptionally helpful friends can also inflate results. Record these biases.
Free work is one possible arrangement, not the definition of a pilot and not
proof of willingness to pay.

## Write the pilot agreement

Define scope, dates, primary contacts, participation, compensation or exchange,
stopping conditions, and how results will be recorded. State what happens if
access or participation is missing. Any contract or use of customer data needs
review appropriate to the jurisdiction and situation.

## Set decision thresholds before starting

Specify the expected observation and a decision rule. For example: three
suitable companies describe the problem without prompting; two accept a short
assessment; one identifies a concrete implementation need. These are
illustrative planning values, not universal benchmarks. Small pilots produce
learning, not statistical certainty.

Record baseline, work performed, intermediate signals, final outcome, and the
customer's qualitative feedback. Ask separately: Was the segment right? Was the
service delivered correctly? Was the result valuable enough to buy? Change the
weakest component, then repeat with comparable cases.

## Pilot record

```text
Case ID and segment:
Fit criteria met / missing:
Baseline and desired outcome:
Agreed scope, timing, and customer contribution:
Actions completed:
Result and supporting evidence:
Deviations and likely causes:
Customer language and objections:
Decision: continue, change, or stop:
Next experiment, owner, and review date:
```

Do not treat a favorable testimonial without an observable result as conclusive
proof. A failed pilot may reflect weak participation, poor selection, a flawed
method, or unrealistic expectations. Diagnose these causes individually before
discarding the entire offer.

## Prepare a relevant work sample

Create a small demonstration: an assessment, sample copy, short analysis,
prototype, or walkthrough. Show the kind of work and why it matters to the
recipient. Do not promise an outcome the sample cannot establish.

Start with well-matched existing customers, past prospects, referrals, and
relationships. Add public directories, professional communities, or events only
where the audience is genuinely present. For every source, ask why suitable
customers should be there, what information is available, and how fit will be
checked. A short qualified list beats a large unfiltered one.

## Outreach structure

Use a truthful, specific reason for contacting each prospect. A first message
should include the observed situation, the pilot service, the effort required
from the prospect, and an easy way to respond. Say that it is a pilot. Do not
imply a prior relationship or fabricate references. Check the applicable contact
rules for the country and channel; `effective-marketing` Outbound Prospecting
owns channel execution.

```text
Subject or opening: [specific, verified observation]

Hi [name],
I noticed [factual observation]. I'm testing [specific approach] for
[defined customer type] facing [problem]. I could show you [small sample
or assessment]. It would require [limited contribution] from your side.
Would that be relevant to your current priorities?
```

Adapt tone, spelling, and level of directness to the recipient and market. A
gatekeeper should hear the real reason for the contact. Do not disguise a sales
pitch as an unrelated administrative question.

## Run and document the pilot

Agree on short checkpoints. A useful demonstration explains the problem, shows a
relevant example, proposes the improvement, and names the required inputs. Treat
it as a testable hypothesis. If sending a document, use a clear filename,
working access, and one sentence describing what the recipient should review.
Open the link yourself before sending.

Document the initial state, objective, actions, result, and limits. Request a
testimonial or permission to publish only after delivering the work. Obtain
explicit approval for each quote, metric, name, and image used. If the customer
contributes too little, clarify expectations and obstacles. Record whether low
participation is likely to be typical of the intended segment; the eventual
offer must accommodate that reality.

## Case-study card

```text
Customer type and starting point:
Goal and agreed scope:
Implementation steps:
Measurement method and period:
Result and limitations:
Exact customer statement, if approved:
Permission for name, quote, metrics, and visuals:
```

To transition to paid work, first review the actual result and remaining goal.
Then propose a defined paid scope. If the pilot did not help, identify and
correct the cause before making a larger offer. Treat a refusal as information
about fit, price, or timing rather than a cue for pressure.
