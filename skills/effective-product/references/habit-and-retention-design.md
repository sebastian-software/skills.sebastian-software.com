# Habit and Retention Design

Use behavioral design when repeated use helps a person realize recurring value
and helps the product sustain a viable relationship. Do not assume every
retention problem needs a habit. Some products are valuable precisely because
they finish a rare task quickly and disappear.

## Check whether a habit fits

Before designing a loop, document:

- the recurring situation or internal state that creates an opportunity
- the progress the person wants in that situation
- the natural frequency of the underlying need
- the smallest action that can produce recognizable value
- what improves through repetition
- what successful completion, pause, or departure looks like
- the business result expected from healthier repetition

If the need is infrequent, consider reminders, re-entry, stored value, trusted
recall, or referral instead of manufacturing daily use. If people return but do
not receive value, fix the product rather than intensifying the prompt.

## Map the habit loop

Use four linked elements:

```text
trigger -> action -> variable reward -> investment -> stronger next trigger
```

Treat the model as an exploration and diagnosis tool, not proof that a mechanic
will work.

### 1. Trigger

External triggers include messages, visible controls, calendar events, social
cues, placement, and paid or owned communication. Internal triggers are
recurring thoughts, emotions, situations, or routines that eventually remind a
person without an external prompt.

Trace a proposed trigger backward:

1. What happened immediately before the person needed the product?
2. What were they trying to avoid, gain, understand, or complete?
3. Which cue already exists in their environment or routine?
4. Can the product become associated with that moment by repeatedly delivering
   value?

Use reminders with understandable timing, source, and controls. Do not infer a
deep emotional vulnerability merely because it could make a stronger message.
Sensitive domains require greater care, especially where distress, financial
pressure, isolation, body image, health, or minors are involved.

### 2. Action

The action is the simplest behavior performed in anticipation of value. Review
it through:

```text
action occurs when motivation, ability, and a timely trigger overlap
```

Improve ability before adding pressure. Check time, money, physical effort,
mental effort, social acceptability, unfamiliarity, device and network
conditions, and accessibility. Preserve enough context that a low-friction
action remains informed rather than accidental.

Motivation can come from hope, relief, curiosity, belonging, mastery, status,
care for others, avoidance of loss, or existing commitment. A legitimate
product may use these motives. Test whether the intensity fits the decision and
whether the person can still understand, decline, undo, or leave.

### 3. Variable reward

Variability can sustain curiosity and discovery when the underlying reward is
valuable. Useful reward families include:

- connection, recognition, and contribution
- resources, opportunities, and useful information
- mastery, progress, completion, and improved capability
- discovery of relevant content or changing real-world conditions

Do not add randomness merely to make checking unpredictable. Define what varies,
why that variation is valuable, and when repetition becomes noise or pressure.
Offer enough predictability that people can form an accurate model and reach a
healthy stopping point.

Rewards can support both user value and business retention. Measure whether
they improve the intended outcome, not only sessions, clicks, or time spent.

### 4. Investment

An investment is work that makes a later experience more valuable or a future
trigger more likely:

- preferences, data, saved items, or learned context
- created content, history, organization, or reputation
- relationships, followers, or collaboration state
- acquired skill, configured workflows, or deliberate commitments

Ask how the investment improves the next use. Distinguish value accumulation
from hostage lock-in. Portability, export, undo, and intelligible consequences
can preserve trust while the product still benefits from personalization and
switching value.

## Use the builder-value matrix

As an introspection prompt, ask two questions:

1. Would the team use this behavior loop themselves or recommend it to someone
   they care about in the same situation?
2. Does the team have credible reason to believe the loop improves the person's
   life or work?

Both yes answers indicate strong alignment. A yes to personal use but uncertain
long-term value suggests entertainment or convenience that should be framed
honestly. Believed value without personal use may be legitimate when the team
is not the target user, but demands direct evidence. Two no answers signal that
the team may be exploiting behavior it neither values nor defends.

This matrix is a nudge, not an ethical certification. Creators can misjudge
value, and target users may reasonably want products the team does not use.

## Distinguish persuasion from unacceptable pressure

Evaluate the mechanism on a spectrum:

- **Transparent persuasion:** clear benefit, accurate claim, informed choice,
  proportionate emotion, and a usable decline or exit.
- **Strong but bounded nudge:** an intentional default, urgency cue, loss frame,
  social signal, or added friction with a real basis and consequences that fit
  the decision.
- **Deception or coercion:** false claims, fake scarcity, concealed costs,
  obstructed exit, confusing consent, accidental commitment, or threats
  unrelated to the person's goal.
- **Material harm:** mechanics that predictably worsen financial, physical,
  psychological, social, or legal outcomes beyond an informed and proportionate
  tradeoff.

Real scarcity may be communicated prominently. Manufactured scarcity may not.
A reminder may be persistent when a person explicitly chose an important goal.
Guilt that attacks identity or relationships to serve a metric is different.
Cancellation may include a clear consequence review; it should not depend on
exhaustion or misdirection.

The line is contextual. Document the claimed user benefit, business benefit,
truth basis, available choice, vulnerable groups, likely harm, and removal
condition instead of relying on a label such as “engagement” or “dark pattern.”

## Run a habit test

1. Define the target behavior and cohort.
2. Find people who already repeat the behavior and those who stopped.
3. Identify the trigger, action, reward, and investment that actually occurred.
4. Compare the path with the proposed mechanism.
5. Change one consequential element or test a small set of distinct loops.
6. Measure first value, repeated value, retention by relevant interval,
   business effect, healthy stopping, complaints, regret, and harm signals.
7. Keep, change, or remove the mechanic at a stated review point.

Do not infer causality from a retained cohort alone. Selection effects, product
quality, seasonality, channel mix, and life circumstances may explain the
pattern. Use experiments or strong comparison where appropriate and pair
behavioral data with direct accounts.

## Diagnose a struggling product

When prior attempts have not improved retention:

- verify that the target group receives the core value at all
- inspect the exact drop-off and the state before it
- separate discoverability, ability, motivation, trust, and timing failures
- generate several distinct loops instead of intensifying the current one
- test truthful triggers, simpler actions, more relevant variability, and
  value-building investments
- protect the critical exit, consent, privacy, payment, and recovery paths

A struggling business is a valid reason to experiment more aggressively. It is
not evidence that a harmful or deceptive mechanic will create durable value.

## Habit-Design Deliverable

Return:

1. recurring situation, desired progress, frequency, and business objective
2. current and proposed trigger-action-reward-investment loops
3. motivation and ability bottlenecks
4. distinct mechanic options with likely user and business effects
5. transparency, choice, vulnerability, and harm review
6. experiment, cohort, metrics, decision window, and removal condition
