# Research and Evidence

Load this reference when a pricing decision depends on willingness to pay,
competitive context, customer segmentation, or evidence that has not yet been
assembled. The goal is not a larger research archive; it is the smallest
credible evidence set that can change the decision.

## Frame the Research Decision

Write the decision in one sentence:

```text
For [customer and buying situation], decide [price, metric, model, package, or
change] by [date] so that [customer and business outcome] while respecting
[constraints].
```

Then name:

- the decision owner and who can approve a test or rollout
- whether the choice is exploratory, reversible, contractual, or difficult to
  reverse
- the target segment, geography, currency, sales motion, and purchase process
- current product promise, alternatives, and switching situation
- which evidence could materially change the recommendation

If the target customer, product promise, or strategic objective is unresolved,
route those decisions to the discovery, strategy, and scope routes before attempting precise pricing.

## Build an Evidence Register

For every important claim, record:

| Field | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Claim | What do we currently believe? |
| Type | Observation, transaction, usage, interview, experiment, market fact, model, stakeholder belief, or unknown? |
| Segment | Which customer situation does it describe? |
| Source | Where did it come from, and can another person inspect it? |
| Date | When was it observed or verified? |
| Strength | Does it show behavior, commitment, stated preference, or only an opinion? |
| Decision use | Which price, metric, package, or risk does it inform? |
| Limitation | What should not be inferred from it? |

Do not average evidence across materially different segments, geographies, or
buying motions merely to obtain one neat number.

## Use Proportionate Evidence

Prefer evidence closer to real choice and commitment:

1. completed purchases, renewals, expansions, churn, negotiated terms, and
   observed switching
2. realistic offer or prototype choices with meaningful consequences
3. past behavior and operational impact described in customer research
4. structured comparison tasks or price-sensitivity exercises
5. hypothetical intent, broad surveys, internal opinions, and generic
   benchmarks

Lower-ranked evidence can generate hypotheses. It should not silently acquire
the certainty of observed transactions.

## Research Willingness to Pay

Investigate the customer's context before asking for a number:

- What problem triggered action, and what happens if nothing changes?
- Which budget, workflow, risk, or revenue line contains the value?
- What alternatives are used today, including manual work and doing nothing?
- Who receives the value, uses the product, pays, approves, and can veto?
- Which usage or outcome changes the value materially?
- What makes the bill feel predictable, fair, or alarming?
- What tradeoffs did customers actually make in previous purchases?

Avoid leading respondents toward the team's preferred model. Ask about recent
behavior before hypothetical prices. When discussing offers, vary meaningful
attributes and make the comparison realistic enough to expose tradeoffs.

No single interview question reveals a true willingness-to-pay number. Treat
method outputs as estimates shaped by framing, sample, product maturity, and
respondent knowledge.

## Research Competitors and Alternatives

Use competitor data to understand the customer's choice set, not to outsource
the decision.

- Verify current public prices, units, allowances, package contents, billing
  periods, currencies, taxes, and update date from inspectable sources.
- Distinguish list price, advertised promotion, negotiated price, and a
  customer's effective total bill.
- Include substitute tools, services, internal labor, risk acceptance, and
  doing nothing.
- Note whether apparent package differences reflect segment needs, sales
  motion, legacy constraints, or unavailable context.
- Do not infer adoption, margins, satisfaction, or transaction prices from a
  public pricing page.

Never fill gaps with plausible-looking competitor numbers. State that a fact
was not verified and explain whether that unknown blocks the decision.

## Design the Smallest Useful Research Step

Choose a method from the uncertainty:

| Uncertainty | Useful next step |
| --- | --- |
| Customer value and alternatives | Behavioral interviews, workflow observation, loss analysis |
| Candidate metric comprehension | Bill walkthroughs with realistic usage histories |
| Package differentiation | Prototype choice tasks and sales-call observation |
| Price sensitivity | Structured comparison, bounded offer test, or actual proposal data |
| Existing-customer risk | Cohort analysis, contract review, support themes, renewal interviews |
| Competitor fact | Current first-party pricing and terms research |

Define in advance:

- who must be represented and which segments must remain separate
- which artifact or offer participants will evaluate
- what will count as supporting, weakening, or invalidating the hypothesis
- what the method cannot establish
- when to stop researching and make a bounded decision

Use qualitative research when traffic or transaction volume cannot support a
credible controlled test. Do not create false statistical authority from a
small or biased sample.

## Report Without False Precision

Return:

1. decision and evidence cutoff
2. supported facts, assumptions, and unknowns
3. segment-specific findings and contradictions
4. plausible price or model ranges only where evidence supports them
5. recommendation at the supported precision
6. next research step and the decision it can change

Avoid universal scores, automatic thresholds, and decimal-level estimates that
the underlying evidence cannot justify.
