# Market and Source Research

Use secondary and proxy sources to understand a market boundary, category,
alternatives, buyer context, language, demand signals, or questions that merit
direct research. Do not turn searchable material into a fictional census of the
market.

## Start with claims, not browsing

Create a claim and source plan:

| Claim or question | Unit and boundary | Evidence needed | Candidate sources | Freshness need | Decision affected |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who experiences the problem? | Role, situation, geography | Observed or reported cases | Interviews, records, communities | Context dependent | Target market |
| What alternatives compete? | Job and decision set | Actual use or consideration | Interviews, reviews, sales records | Current | Product and position |
| How large is the opportunity? | Buyers, users, accounts, spend, or events | Count, rate, or transparent model | Public data, filings, licensed data | Dated | Viability |
| What language appears? | Source and participant context | Verbatim material | Calls, tickets, reviews, forums | Recent enough for use | Research and messaging |

State which result would change the decision before opening a search engine.

## Map the market around customer choice

Describe:

- target situation and desired progress
- buyer, user, payer, approver, administrator, and blocker
- current workflow and doing-nothing alternative
- internal build, manual service, adjacent tool, direct product, and substitute
- trigger, evaluation criteria, switching cost, adoption constraint, and exit
- route to awareness, evaluation, purchase, implementation, and repeated value

Treat vendor categories and analyst taxonomies as hypotheses. Customers may
compare products across categories or compare a product with a spreadsheet,
consultant, internal process, delay, or no action.

## Build a source portfolio

Prefer the most direct and authoritative source available for each claim:

1. first-party operational records, observed behavior, and direct accounts
2. official statistics, filings, standards, registries, and primary research
3. current vendor documentation, pricing, terms, and product behavior
4. licensed analyst or industry data with visible method and scope
5. reviews, support, sales, community, search, and social evidence
6. commentary, roundups, and unattributed estimates

Authority alone does not establish relevance. Record method, population,
geography, date, definitions, commercial incentives, and known exclusions.
Triangulate consequential claims across sources that do not merely repeat the
same upstream estimate.

## Use reviews and communities as proxy evidence

Reviews, forums, comments, public complaints, support records, sales notes, job
postings, and search behavior can expose:

- vocabulary and problem framing
- recent triggers and workarounds
- alternatives and evaluation criteria
- failure, adoption, switching, and churn contexts
- questions for direct research

Preserve URL or record ID, date, product and plan context, available participant
context, quote or observation, and theme tag. Name the selection effect:

- reviews overrepresent people willing and able to review;
- support records overrepresent reportable problems;
- sales notes reflect a buying conversation and the recorder's incentives;
- technical communities often overrepresent expert or skeptical perspectives;
- social metrics reflect platform exposure and ranking as well as interest;
- search volume reflects queries captured by a provider, not complete demand.

Do not treat repeated wording as population frequency without a defensible
denominator. Use proxy material to refine hypotheses, recruitment, language,
and direct-research questions.

## Research competitors through evidence

Separate:

- currently verified product behavior, package, price, terms, and availability
- customer-reported experience with known source and date
- market interpretation and positioning
- assumptions, rumors, and missing access

Review customers' decision sets, not only the competitors named by the team.
Include manual, internal, adjacent, and no-action alternatives. Verify volatile
facts in current primary sources where possible. Do not infer strategy,
financial health, customer count, roadmap, or universal satisfaction from a
marketing page or a few reviews.

Return competitor and alternative evidence to the discovery, strategy, and scope routes for product
strategy and to `effective-marketing` for category, positioning, messaging, and
sales decisions.

## Size a market transparently

Define the unit before calculating: people, organizations, seats, transactions,
workflows, spend, or another decision-relevant quantity. State geography,
period, segment, eligibility, adoption condition, price or value assumption,
and whether the estimate concerns total, serviceable, or reachable opportunity.

Prefer a transparent model:

```text
eligible units
× observed or bounded incidence
× realistic reach or adoption condition
× relevant annual value
= scenario, not fact
```

Use ranges and scenarios when inputs are uncertain. Keep sourced observations
separate from team assumptions. Reconcile top-down and bottom-up estimates by
explaining definition differences rather than averaging them into false
precision. A large market does not establish access, urgency, authority,
willingness to pay, or product fit.

## Preserve freshness and reproducibility

For each consequential fact, record:

- exact claim
- source and direct link or record identifier
- publisher or owner
- publication and observation date
- method and scope
- excerpt, field, or calculation used
- access restriction
- corroborating or contradicting source
- next verification date or change trigger

If current information cannot be accessed, report the gap and the weaker claim
that the available evidence permits. Never silently substitute memory.

## Source-research handoff

Return:

1. market and question boundary
2. source register and search cutoff
3. category, actor, alternative, and decision-path map
4. supported facts and transparent calculations
5. customer-language and proxy-evidence register
6. contradictions, selection effects, and missing perspectives
7. claims requiring direct research or current verification
