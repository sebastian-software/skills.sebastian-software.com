# Persuasive Nonfiction

Persuasive nonfiction helps a reader make sense of a real choice. It may also
build authority, demand, or commercial interest. The persuasive effect should
come from relevance, specificity, proof, and judgment—not from hiding the
writer's interest or manufacturing pressure.

## Define the persuasive job

Write down:

- the reader and situation
- the change or problem already recognizable to that reader
- the belief, choice, or next step the piece should make more reasonable
- what the author, organization, or product can credibly contribute
- the strongest supplied proof
- the main objection or alternative
- what remains uncertain

Decide how explicit the commercial intent should be. A useful article may earn
attention and trust without pitching throughout. A product description should
make the offer and next step unmistakable. Do not disguise an advertisement as
independent analysis.

## Build a credible argument

A practical sequence is:

1. Name a recognizable situation or consequential tension.
2. Offer a specific claim, interpretation, or useful distinction.
3. Explain the mechanism or reasoning.
4. Support it with supplied examples, evidence, experience, or demonstration.
5. Treat a serious objection or boundary fairly.
6. Show what changes for the reader.
7. Invite a proportionate next step.

The call to action should match the evidence and relationship. Reading a
related guide, trying a reversible exercise, replying with an experience, or
requesting a conversation may be more credible than an immediate purchase.

## Use proof accurately

Useful proof may include:

- measured before-and-after outcomes with context
- a case study that names starting conditions, work, result, and limits
- direct product behavior or demonstration
- relevant professional experience clearly identified as experience
- a supplied customer quotation with permission and attribution
- a reasoned example or counterexample
- external research that can be checked and cited

Do not turn one case into a universal result, correlation into causation,
internal estimates into observed outcomes, or a polished anecdote into a
testimonial. Keep time period, sample, segment, and comparison details when
they affect the claim.

## Thought leadership and content marketing

Strong thought leadership contributes judgment, not just information. It may:

- name a pattern others have not articulated clearly
- challenge a common practice with evidence and a usable alternative
- connect developments across fields
- show how a decision was made, including tradeoffs and changed beliefs
- teach a framework through a concrete case
- make the author's operating principles visible

Avoid borrowed certainty, generic trend summaries, and provocative claims that
exist only to attract reaction. A piece can market the writer effectively by
demonstrating how they notice, reason, and work.

## Case studies

Use a traceable sequence:

1. customer or project context that can be disclosed
2. prior state, constraint, and desired outcome
3. why the chosen response fit
4. what was actually done
5. supplied result and measurement window
6. limitations, dependencies, and what transferred to later work
7. appropriate next step

If results or permission are missing, write a project narrative or method
example instead of inventing a success story.

## About pages and professional narratives

An About page should help the intended reader understand:

- who is speaking
- which problems or people they care about
- what experience supports that focus
- how they tend to work or decide
- what makes the relationship relevant now

Select details for relevance and character. Avoid a chronological résumé in
paragraph form, unsupported superlatives, and synthetic vulnerability. Personal
material should reveal a meaningful connection to the work.

## Product and service descriptions

Connect:

```text
recognizable situation -> relevant capability -> resulting progress -> proof
```

Describe what the offer does, for whom, under which conditions, and what the
reader can expect next. Translate features into consequences without erasing
important constraints. Use sensory or emotional language only when it matches
the actual experience.

Urgency is legitimate when the underlying constraint is real and material,
such as a disclosed capacity limit, application window, or remaining stock.
Fake countdowns, invented scarcity, hidden renewal, and ambiguous pricing
undermine the factual contract.

## Titles, leads, and calls to action

- A title should promise the actual value of the piece.
- A lead may foreground the reader's situation, a useful contradiction, or a
  specific observation.
- Curiosity is useful when the answer is delivered promptly.
- A call to action should say what happens next and why that step fits.
- Multiple calls to action should have a clear primary choice.

Do not substitute formulas for relevance. If a hook sounds stronger than the
evidence, weaken the hook rather than stretching the body to justify it.

## Persuasion Check

- Is the commercial or reputational interest handled honestly?
- Does the piece give the reader something useful before asking for action?
- Are claims specific, supportable, and appropriately qualified?
- Is the strongest objection addressed rather than caricatured?
- Does the author demonstrate judgment instead of merely claiming authority?
- Is the next step proportionate to the evidence and relationship?
- Would the piece remain defensible if a skeptical customer examined every
  claim?
