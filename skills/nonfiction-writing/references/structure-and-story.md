# Structure and Story

Use structure to control what the reader understands, when they understand it,
and why the next paragraph feels necessary. Structure is not a template pasted
onto the material; it is the visible shape of the writer's reasoning.

## Find the governing idea

Complete these sentences before outlining:

- This piece is about ...
- More specifically, it argues or reveals ...
- The reader needs this now because ...
- After reading, the reader should understand, believe, feel, or do ...
- The piece will not try to cover ...

The governing idea should be narrower than the topic. “AI and product
management” is a subject. “Cheap AI prototypes should widen exploration without
being mistaken for customer evidence” is a workable governing idea.

Every substantial section should advance, complicate, demonstrate, or apply
that idea. Interesting material that does none of those jobs belongs elsewhere.

## Choose a useful reading path

Select a shape that matches the material:

- **Explanation:** question or confusion → necessary context → mechanism →
  example → implication
- **Argument:** consequential claim → evidence → reasoning → strongest fair
  objection → qualified conclusion
- **Problem and response:** recognizable situation → cost or tension → causes →
  available choices → recommended response
- **Case study:** prior state → constraint → decision → work → measured result →
  limits and transferable lesson
- **Personal essay:** specific experience → tension or surprise → reflection →
  widened meaning without pretending one experience is universal
- **Profile or place:** revealing scene → selected detail and context → change or
  contradiction → earned portrait
- **How-to:** desired result → prerequisites → ordered actions → checks and
  failure recovery → next level

Hybrids are normal. Name the main spine so side paths do not take over.

## Design the lead

A lead creates orientation and forward movement. It may begin with:

- a specific scene, action, or observation
- a useful contradiction
- a question the piece will genuinely answer
- a concrete consequence for the reader
- a compact claim whose support follows promptly
- an unfamiliar detail that reveals the larger subject

Supply enough context for the opening to mean something. Avoid throat-clearing,
generic declarations of importance, invented drama, and mystery that withholds
basic facts only to simulate suspense.

Drafting the lead early can expose the piece's direction, but revise it after
the body exists. The final lead must introduce the piece that was actually
written.

## Build sections and transitions

Give each section one dominant job. For every paragraph, ask:

1. What does the reader know at the start?
2. What new thing does this paragraph add?
3. What sentence carries the paragraph's center of gravity?
4. Why does the next paragraph follow?

Transitions need not announce themselves with “however” or “therefore.” They
can work through repeated key terms, cause and effect, time, contrast, a
question and answer, a change in scale, or the consequence of the previous
paragraph.

Use headings when they help readers navigate a long or task-oriented piece.
Do not use headings to hide a sequence of unrelated notes.

## Work with people, places, and personal material

- Prefer selective, revealing details over inventories of appearance or
  atmosphere.
- Show what a person did, chose, made, avoided, or said in context.
- Quote exact supplied language when its wording matters; otherwise paraphrase
  faithfully.
- Identify where memory is approximate or where another participant might
  reasonably remember the event differently.
- Let a place affect action or meaning rather than serving as decorative
  scenery.
- Move from an individual case to a general lesson carefully. One vivid story
  demonstrates possibility, not prevalence.
- Protect confidential or sensitive detail that is unnecessary to the point.

## Handle complexity

For technical, institutional, or unfamiliar subjects:

- start from the reader's likely question, not the writer's research chronology
- define only the terms needed for the next step
- use an example before adding a second abstraction
- distinguish mechanism, analogy, and evidence
- surface conditions, exceptions, and uncertainty near the claim they qualify
- return periodically to why the detail matters

Do not simplify by removing the decisive complication. Simplify the route into
the complexity.

## End with earned force

An ending can:

- show the consequence of the argument
- return to an opening image with changed meaning
- name the decision or next action now available
- offer a final specific observation
- state a qualified synthesis that the evidence has earned

Avoid introducing a major unsupported claim at the end. Remove recap that only
repeats headings, inspirational uplift disconnected from the material, and
multiple endings that compete for the last word.

## Structure Check

- Can the governing idea be stated in one sentence?
- Does the lead create both orientation and movement?
- Does every section have a necessary job?
- Are evidence and context placed before claims that rely on them?
- Does the sequence handle the strongest likely reader question?
- Are examples representative of what the text claims?
- Does the ending complete the piece without merely stopping?
